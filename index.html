Taiwanese Mandarin Text-to-Speech

IndexTTS2 is an open-source model that turns text into natural-sounding speech. It only read Simplified Chinese and English, and it had no Taiwanese accent. I adapted it to read Traditional Chinese and to speak the way people in Taiwan do.

  • Python
  • PyTorch
  • LoRA
  • Docker
Code on GitHub ↗

The problem

Text in Taiwan is written in Traditional Chinese. Over a third of the characters in the Taiwanese training data (1,180 of 3,315) weren't in the model's vocabulary. It also had no way to choose between the different readings of a character, and it came with no code for training it further.

I also measured how often the accent shows up in 33.6 hours of Taiwanese recordings. Only 2.7% of characters are pronounced differently from standard Mandarin, but 48.6% of sentences with Chinese text contain at least one of them.

The order matters. The word splitter was trained on Simplified text, so converting first gives cleaner word boundaries, and the right reading depends on the word.

Teaching it the accent

Getting the accent was the easy part. Keeping each speaker's own voice at the same time was the hard part.

Fine-tune
Trained the model on only the Taiwanese recordings. The accent sounded natural, but every voice came out female, even when the reference voice was male.
LoRA
Trained small add-on layers instead of the whole model. The accent was strong, but voices still drifted toward the training speakers. Switching LoRA on for different parts of the model showed that, for one voice, the accent lives mostly in the lower layers.
LoRA + GRL
Added a second network that penalizes the model for learning who is speaking, so it only learns the accent. The mechanism worked, but training didn't settle and the output lost the accent. Next idea: train in phases so the new network starts from a stable point.

Speech-to-speech prototype

  1. ListenWhisper turns speech into text
  2. ReplyOllama writes an answer
  3. SpeakIndexTTS2 reads it aloud
  4. ServeDocker + Flask on the local network

It works end to end, but it takes about 2.2 seconds to generate each second of speech, and the GPU only reached 25% use even after speed-ups like half-precision and caching. Getting it under real time is the main open problem.

Ultrasonic Directional Speaker

A speaker that sends sound in a narrow beam, so only the person standing in front of it can hear it. I started from an open-source design, went through it part by part, and redrew the preamp and driver in KiCad to fix the problems I found.

  • KiCad
  • STM32
  • Analog circuits

How it works

The speaker plays audio on top of a 40 kHz ultrasonic signal, which is too high for people to hear. Ultrasound travels in a tight beam, and as it moves through the air, the air itself turns it back into sound you can hear. So the audio is only audible inside the beam.

  1. Audio in3.5 mm stereo jack
  2. PreampMCP6002, centered at 1.65 V
  3. MicrocontrollerSTM32 reads audio, outputs 40 kHz PWM
  4. DriverMIC4427, 18 V
  5. Transducers40 kHz array

The bug that started the redesign

While tracing the original circuit, I found that the audio reaching the STM32 could dip below 0 V.

Problem
The preamp output went through a capacitor before the STM32's analog input. A capacitor blocks steady voltage, so after it the audio swings around 0 V and half of every wave goes negative.
Why it matters
The STM32 can only measure 0 to 3.3 V. Anything below 0 V gets cut off, which distorts the sound, and pulling a pin below ground can stress it.
Fix
I removed that capacitor and held the op-amp at 1.65 V, half of 3.3 V, so the audio swings around the middle of the range the STM32 can read. I also replaced the LM358 running on 5 V with an MCP6002 running on 3.3 V. Its output can't go above 3.3 V, so it can't overshoot the input either.

Other changes from the original

  • Wired the stereo jack properly and mixed left and right, so a stereo plug no longer shorts the right channel.
  • Made the gain adjustable (about 0.5× to 5.5×) instead of a fixed 100×, so different audio sources don't clip.
  • Added a filter at the STM32's input that cuts sound above about 16 kHz before it gets sampled.
  • Gave the preamp its own filtered supply to keep digital noise and hum out of the signal.
  • Added supply capacitors at the driver, plus pull-down resistors so the transducers stay quiet while the STM32 boots.

Smaller projects

    500 MHz Bandpass Filter

    The task was to let a 500 MHz signal from an antenna through and block everything else. I chained three inductor–capacitor circuits that each resonate at 500 MHz, linked by small capacitors, and simulated it in LTspice with ideal parts. The linking capacitors shift each circuit's tuning, so I trimmed each circuit's own capacitor to bring all three back to exactly 500 MHz. I also built a two-stage version to compare. At 450 MHz, less than 0.4% of the signal's voltage gets through.

    • Passes 490–513 MHz
    • −49 dB at 450 MHz
    • −38 dB at 550 MHz

    ROS/GSH-Responsive Nanoparticles

    An independent research proposal for a nanoparticle that holds on to its cargo until it reaches cells with high levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) or glutathione (GSH), two chemical signals that run high in many diseased cells. Those molecules cut a self-immolative linker, which then falls apart on its own and releases the payload. In the lab itself, I worked with the team testing proteins as carriers for delivering Cas9 RNP into neurons.
